01

A recipe, four families, one retained world

How to read the app and this atlas.

Where it started. Magna Urbis began as an exploration of Watabou’s Medieval Fantasy City Generator (Oleg Dolya, source code TownGeneratorOS): a town grown from a market, wards cut by streets, walls and a countryside, drawn as a clean plan. That generator is the first inspiration of this project, and its plain map look lives on as the “Plain (classic)” variant of the cadastre style. Magna Urbis then took the idea down to the terrain, the water and the region, and up to every house.

A Magna Urbis world is described by a recipe: the seed, the map size, the relief source and every setting. The same recipe on the same engine gives the same world, which is why a share link or a saved project is enough to rebuild a map.

The app runs the recipe as stages grouped in four families. The region builds the ground, its water and its natural cover. Settlements place towns, villages, farms and the roads between them. Cities build one town at a time, from its footprint to its roofs. The map draws all of it in one of seven styles, in 2D or 3D. Each family keeps its products, so changing a setting reruns only the stages downstream of it, and changing the look of the map never reruns generation.

How the stages are documented

Every stage in the app has a card in this atlas, under the chapter that explains it. A card says what the stage computes and why, the method actually used in the code, its inputs and outputs, the controls and their visible effect, and where it runs. Optional stages can be switched off in the app; required ones cannot.

A note on claims. Magna Urbis combines published algorithms with its own heuristics. A plausible river is not a hydrological forecast, a medina plan is not an archaeological reconstruction, and a requested population is a target, not a census. Where a result is an approximation, the chapter says so.

Relief: three sources, one surface

Procedural landscape evolution, neural diffusion or an imported heightmap.

The relief is a signed height field in metres above sea level, kept at a fixed physical resolution for the whole run. It comes from exactly one of three sources, and all three end in the same shared finishing chain, so everything downstream reads one kind of surface.

Procedural relief

Perlin (2002) gives the coherent gradient noise this source starts from, and Tricard et al. (2019) the phasor noise used for oriented detail. The procedural source starts from coherent noise, shaped by optional painted zones (plain, hills, mountains, high mountains). Zones are masks that steer the operators; they never set an elevation directly. Seeded volcanoes and calderas add lobed rims, breaches and nested vents.

The incision solver is the implicit stream-power method of Braun and Willett (2013). The heart of the chain is a bounded landscape evolution on the regional grid: each cycle applies tectonic uplift, routes water downhill, incises channels with a stream-power law, then relaxes slopes with thermal collapse and diffusion. The result is reconstructed on the fine grid with a domain warp, calibrated to the amplitude of the chosen relief family, given ridged or phasor-noise detail and a last pass of surface erosion. Since November, every surface operator scales with the cell size, so the same recipe gives the same landforms on a 5 km or a 50 km map.

Where the coast is cut

The land–sea cut is a stage of its own that can be moved along the chain: right after the initial noise by default, or later, after relaxation, calibration, detail, surface erosion or datum normalisation. Whatever happens after it, the finishing chain reapplies the geographic cut so the chosen coastline survives, then erodes it.

Imported heightmaps

An imported or library heightmap is resampled into the frame, scaled vertically, given a datum and cropped. It skips the generative stages but goes through the same finishing.

Regional evolutionrelief-regionalLets rivers and slopes organise the surface: uplift raises ground, water routes along the steepest path and cuts valleys, hillsides slump, and diffusion softens what is left.
Procedural reliefRequired
What and why
Lets rivers and slopes organise the surface: uplift raises ground, water routes along the steepest path and cuts valleys, hillsides slump, and diffusion softens what is left. After a few cycles the noise has become a connected drainage with ridges, valleys and fans.
Method
Each cycle is a dependent sequence. Uplift; Priority-Flood (Barnes et al.) drainage from the outlets by increasing height, with sills of closed basins carved by a fraction (0.1) of their depth per step while basins fill with sediment (multiplier 3); flow accumulation split between two downhill neighbours on triangular facets (D-infinity, Tarboton); implicit stream-power incision, dh/dt = U - K A^m S^n, solved downstream first after Braun and Willett, with capped contribution and damped response on gentle slopes; thermal relaxation above the talus angle; hillslope diffusion. Graph convergence is checked and retried rather than assumed after a fixed number of relaxations.
In and out
In: initial heights and uplift. Out: evolved regional heights, drainage area and flow directions.
Controls
Uplift, regional thermal and diffusion rates, biome, cycle count; the climate preset scales hydraulic incision and thermal rates. Thermal and diffusion can be disabled separately; drainage and incision cannot, because incision reads the flow data.
Runs on
Dominates the procedural cost, which grows as cycles times regional cells. Native Rust solver (Priority-Flood lives in the terrain crate) or WGSL kernels, whose topologies may differ. The erosion dose is an intensity, not years, and sediment mass is not conserved globally.

Neural relief

A diffusion model conditioned on climate and an optional sketch.

The entry point of the neural relief is Infinite Generation: Alexander Goslin’s InfiniteDiffusion and Terrain Diffusion, described in InfiniteDiffusion: Bridging Learned Fidelity and Procedural Utility for Open-World Terrain Generation (arXiv 2512.08309). Its idea is what makes a learned terrain usable in a procedural world: windows generated lazily, consistent with the seed and independent of the visiting order, so the terrain has no edge and no seams. The models Magna Urbis runs are distilled from the Terrain Diffusion models and follow the method of that paper; the distillation and the browser runtime are developed in Neural Earth.

Like Terrain Diffusion, the models use the diffusion formulation of Karras et al. (2022, 2024). The neural source samples a three-stage diffusion model trained on real elevation data. A coarse stage at 7.68 km per cell is conditioned on the climate (temperature, precipitation and their variability) and on an optional sketch. A base stage refines it to 240 m, and a decoder brings it to 30 m with an optional amplification that adds a residual on top of the retained spline. Each stage is sampled in global tile coordinates with a halo, so tiles join without seams.

The model runs in the browser through Rust compiled to WebAssembly with WebGPU, or in the optional local Python service with CUDA. Only the decoded terrain is exposed; the intermediate stages have no separate preview.

Limit. A learned model reproduces the statistics of its training data. It does not simulate geology, and its output is not a measured place.

Finishing the surface, and the world below it

Shared operators every relief goes through, and the optional underdark layer.

Whatever the source, four operators finish the surface. Fractal refinement adds slope-dependent detail below the source cell size, with its own Hurst exponent, lacunarity and octaves. Fluvial conditioning breaches or erodes depressions so water can leave the land. Wind transport moves material along an azimuth for dunes and loess. Coastal erosion shapes the chosen shoreline.

Underdark

The underdark is a world layer, not a stage: when chosen, it carves the finished surface once, before hydrology, into a cave world. The relief becomes the rock above; drainage becomes the tunnel network.

Water: drainage, lakes, rivers and mouths

A drainage graph and a water budget, then vector rivers.

Drainage relies on the Priority-Flood algorithm of Barnes, Lehman and Mulla (2014). Water is computed once on the finished surface by the Rust vector water chain. It first conditions the relief so that every cell can drain, by breaching passes up to 8 m deep with valley-shaped or stepped banks. A Priority-Flood pass then gives every cell a downhill direction and every depression a spill point.

Lakes follow the fill, spill and merge logic of Barnes, Callaghan and Wickert (2021), fed by the annual water balance of Turc (1954). Depressions are where lakes may form, but only if the climate can feed them. For each closed basin the chain computes a water budget from temperature, altitude and precipitation; a basin keeps a lake only where runoff can sustain it and where depth, area and coverage tests pass. The rest are breached or filled. The lake abundance setting moves those thresholds.

Rivers that behave

Once lakes are decided, rivers that pass beside a lake are captured into it, and upstream areas are summed again so discharge stays consistent. River heads can extend up the talweg, spurs shorter than 150 m are dropped, and vector rivers receive a width, a bed and meanders.

Mouth widening follows the exponential estuary shape of Savenije (2005). Mouths are shaped last. A river widens towards the sea or a lake along an exponential profile, as estuaries do, without ever narrowing again, and its water surface ends at sea level without rising seaward. Large rivers on gentle coasts may open into a funnel or tidal estuary.

Limit. The budget is annual and static. There are no floods, no groundwater and no seasonal regime; river ice and snow are drawn by the renderer from the season, not simulated.

Settlements and the countryside

Where places go, how large they are, and what surrounds them.

Places are chosen on the finished region. Every dry cell gets a site score (water, slope, coast, river crossings, defence), and places are picked greedily with a minimum spacing, each one carving an urban reserve. You can ask for a number of places drawn from a size law, or give an explicit list; a listed place that lands in water is moved to the nearest suitable dry site with a warning, so a list is always honoured.

The size rule is Zipf’s rank-size law (1949). Sizes follow a rank-size law: the place of rank r holds the population of the largest divided by rq, down to a minimum. The number of ranks follows how many places the usable land can hold.

The countryside

Farms are placed within the farmland reach of their parent place, with a few isolated ones farther out. Each takes a farm type from its culture, from the Austrian four-sided farm to the Breton longère, the Apulian masseria, the Japanese minka or the southern African kraal, with its own ground plan and massing. Cultivated land follows the population and stays within walking distance of the farmers. Hamlets and villages get a chapel just outside, and wayside shrines mark junctions. Small places are built in the background after a run.

Remote places

Rare sites are chosen by situation: summits, crags, cliff ledges, gorges, islets, deep forest or marsh islands. Their access is a cliff stair, rock steps, a causeway, a rope bridge, a tunnel or a track, and an access line that meets a road ends on it rather than leaving a spur.

Regional roads

Which places to link, then how a road actually crosses the land.

Roads are built in two steps: decide which places to connect, then find physical paths. The candidate links are a Delaunay graph of the places, reduced to a minimum spanning tree, plus shortcuts where the tree makes a long detour.

Routing follows Galin, Peytavie, Maréchal and Guérin (2010): a least-cost path over the terrain with an extended set of moves. Each link is then routed by a least-cost search over the terrain. A move can go to any of 24 neighbours (the 8 adjacent cells, knight moves and longer (1, 3) moves), and the search state includes the arrival heading, so turns, slopes and river crossings all have a price. The most important trips are routed first. Reusing an existing road is cheaper than building one, and cells right beside a road cost more than the road itself, so routes either share a trunk or keep a clear distance.

All paths are written into one graph whose vertices are grid cells pinned to places or map exits. Crossings become junctions, small triangles lose their longest side, and chains of degree-two vertices become links that take the class of the most important trip using them. Surface follows the development level and the culture. Toll houses, ferries, inns and border posts are read from the finished network.

The town plan

Footprint, macroplan, streets and quarters, then trimming.

A town is built when you select it, one at a time or all together, by the Rust engine. The chain reads the region, the road graph and the culture of the place.

Kind and footprint

The town's kind (from a lone building or a ruin up to a metropolis) follows its population, culture, site and settlement form. Its footprint spends a land budget on a bounded raster with an anisotropic arrival field, so towns stretch along valleys, roads and shores. A survey of the terrain picks how much slope the plan may accept before the first layout, so mountain towns climb instead of failing.

Neighbouring towns

Towns are generated in a fixed order, largest first. Each town receives the claims of the earlier towns near it, the land they occupy and their edge streets: it keeps off that land, joins those streets, moves its centre if needed and warns when land runs short.

Macroplan, streets and quarters

The macroplan is geography first: it keeps regional road corridors, partitions the dry land, places harbours on waterfronts rooted on dry ground, and lays out walls, gates and cultural phases (core, ring, faubourgs).

Street guidance by a direction field comes from Chen, Esch, Wonka, Müller and Zhang (2008), within the procedural city chain introduced by Parish and Müller (2001). Streets are cut recursively inside each quarter, guided by a direction field, and routed locally around obstacles. Quarters are computed in parallel with one random stream each, so the output does not depend on the number of threads. Streets never lie on water: a wet stretch becomes a bridge deck or stops short of the bank.

Fortifications follow the culture: walls with towers, gates, bastions and ditches, palisades or hedges. A wall can extend an older one, castles take knolls, and walled towns are filled up to the wall street. Springs inside a town receive a water building chosen by its culture, and some cultures add informal quarters at the edge.

Trim

The first plan is generous. Trimming runs a cheap probe of the cadastre, turns blocks that would stay empty into fields or open land and prunes the lanes that served them, until nothing changes (at most eight passes), then runs a door pass after bridges. Loose bank streets, junction triangles and doubled runs are removed.

The parcel operators follow Vanegas, Kelly, Weber et al. (2012). Each block is divided into parcels that front a street, with four cutting operators chosen by the culture (strips, perimeter blocks, courts, compounds) and variants for organic towns, camps, ruins and monuments. A parcel must touch an access segment over a real length and contain buildable land; spare cells are merged into occupied neighbours. Parcel ids survive tidying, so edits stick.

The programme then assigns uses. Demand comes from the population and the role tables of the culture: landmarks and large programmes reserve whole blocks first, then trades and shops take street lots, then housing, and leftovers become gardens and yards. Household size is culture data, not a constant. No more than 3 % of the plan may turn housing blocks into commons, parks or cemeteries.

A large town produces a lot of data. Parcels can be returned as JSON or as a compact binary pack; for a town of a million people the pack weighs 151 MB against 536 MB of JSON.

Seven styles, one recipe per pass, seasons, and two drawing engines.

Each of eleven climate zones (temperate, Mediterranean, continental, Nordic, alpine, Arctic, steppe, desert, savanna, tropical, wetland) has a calendar of four seasonal rows: herb tint, leaves, snow, thaw and sea ice. The palette of every vector style follows it, the shaders read the same calendar, and you can edit the year of a zone. Snow is a continuous cover shaped by relief, rivers and lakes freeze only where the ground does, and sea ice has leads, floes and slush. On parchment, seasons change the land use only.

Two engines

The GPU vector engine applies the stencil-then-cover path rendering of Kilgard and Bolz (2012). The ground is drawn by a WebGL2 or WebGPU terrain renderer. Everything vector (water, roads, parcels, buildings, symbols, text) goes through one drawing interface with two back ends: a Canvas 2D engine on the CPU, and a GPU engine that records the drawing calls once into a display list and replays it every frame with the view matrix, using stencil-then-cover fills, extruded strokes and instanced sprites. A band around the view is recorded at each half octave of zoom, so panning and zooming need no new tiles. The GPU engine falls back to the CPU on software GL, a lost context or slow frames.

The 3D view uses WebGPU, with WebGL2 as fallback, and drapes the 2D map on the relief, raises building volumes by sector, and can add trees within 360 m of the camera, which follow the season. You can orbit, walk or fly.

Where it runs

The browser, a native engine on the same machine, and an optional neural service.

The interface and the drawing run on the main thread. Generation runs in a worker that queues requests and keeps the world; rendering, composition, export, occupation and road routing have their own workers. The computation itself is Rust, compiled two ways.

  • WebAssembly, in the browser: multi-threaded when the page is cross-origin isolated, otherwise single-threaded or as a pool of single-threaded workers.
  • The native engine, magna-urbis-local on port 8791: relief, water, coast, land use, forest, site choice, roads and towns, with one session per town lane and binary replies. The desktop app starts it automatically on a free port.
  • The neural service, optional, in Python with CUDA on port 8793. The neural relief also runs in the browser with WebGPU.

Open limits and what comes next

What the generator does not do yet.

  • Towns do not grow. A town is planned at its final size. Growth by accretion, where a larger town keeps the plan of its smaller self, is designed but not built; only a chronology hook exists.
  • A million is a ceiling for the pages. The engine generates a town of a million people, but neither page can receive it yet; districts on demand are not implemented.
  • Water is annual. No floods, groundwater or seasonal flow; winter is drawn, not simulated.
  • Cultures are data, not history. A culture sheet is an artistic synthesis from sources and references; it is not a reconstruction of a real town.
  • The backend is part of the recipe. CPU and GPU paths are not promised bit-identical; the same recipe on another engine may differ in detail.

Glossary

Each term with one meaning in this atlas.

Recipe
Seed, size, sources and every setting of a world. Rebuilds it on the same engine.
Retained product
A stage output kept in memory and reused by later stages and views until a setting upstream changes.
Priority-Flood
An algorithm that gives every cell of a height field a drainage order and every depression a spill point.
Water budget
Annual precipitation minus evapotranspiration over a basin; decides whether a lake can be kept.
Talweg
The line joining the lowest points of a valley.
Rank-size law
Place r has the population of the largest divided by rq.
Footprint
The land given to a town, distinct from its streets and buildings.
Macroplan
The geographic frame of a town: corridors, harbours, walls, phases.
Block, parcel
Land enclosed by streets; a property in it that fronts a street.
Programme
The uses demanded by the population and their allocation to parcels.
Massing
Raising each planned piece of a building into a volume with floors, eaves and a roof.
Culture sheet
The data describing one architectural culture: streets, plots, houses, monuments, countryside.
Style sheet
A hybrid mix of cultures, weighted facet by facet.
Display list
Recorded drawing calls that the GPU replays every frame.
